# Rubric: instructional

**Stage:** STORYBOARD. **Lens:** does each module actually teach: clear explanations, well-chosen examples, scenarios that exercise real decisions, and practice placed where it helps?

## Read

1. `storyboard/storyboard.json`, module by module, in reading order.
2. The design's module plans and objectives, for intent.

## Checks

- [ ] **Explanations.** Each concept is explained before it is used, in plain language, with the literal meaning after any analogy. A learner could answer "why does this matter?" from the text.
- [ ] **One purpose per block.** Blocks do one job; long blocks are split where a learner would lose the thread; tiny blocks that fragment one idea are merged.
- [ ] **Worked examples and comparisons.** Abstract principles are followed by a concrete example from the learners' context; comparisons state the dimension being compared.
- [ ] **Scenarios.** Scenarios present a realistic situation with enough information to decide, a genuine decision point, and consequences or feedback that teach. "Not enough information yet, escalate" is allowed as a correct outcome where the evidence supports it. Continuing cases stay consistent across modules.
- [ ] **Misconceptions addressed.** Planned misconceptions are named and corrected with reasons, not just contradicted.
- [ ] **Practice placement.** Formative items follow the teaching they practise, retrieve earlier material where the design planned spacing, and their feedback teaches.
- [ ] **Transfer.** Modules connect content to what learners will do or escalate at work; the review integrates across modules.
- [ ] **Cognitive load.** New terms and models arrive one at a time; dense passages are broken with structure (lists, steps) or a visual.
- [ ] **Boundaries.** Escalation and "not qualified to" statements appear at the decision points where learners need them, not only in the introduction.
- [ ] **Treatment notes.** `treatment` explains the instructional intent well enough for a reviewer to judge whether the block achieves it.

## Severity calibration

- `critical`: a core concept used in graded items but never explained; a scenario whose "correct" path teaches an unsafe or wrong decision.
- `major`: explanations that assert without explaining; scenarios with no real decision; misconceptions ignored; practice before teaching.
- `minor`: an example that could be more concrete; a block that should be split.
- `style`: phrasing (leave to editorial).

## Not your job

Missing design content (content-completeness), item construction (assessment), citations (citation-evidence), accessibility, prose polish (editorial-readability).

## Categories

`instructional`, `scenario`, `cognitive-load`
