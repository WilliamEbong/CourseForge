# 1. Course title and design summary

## Course title

**Laboratory Chemical Risk, Incident Investigation, and CAPA**

## Design summary

This course is designed for educated, nontechnical professionals who work in or around laboratory operations but are not assumed to be chemists, occupational hygienists, incident investigators, or regulated-quality specialists. The learning sequence follows the real decision flow of laboratory risk management rather than the order of legislation or the structure of a safety manual:

**identify the hazard → understand the task → assess exposure and risk → choose controls → recognize when work or conditions have changed → respond and escalate when something goes wrong → investigate causes → select corrective actions → verify effectiveness → feed learning back into the system.**

The course begins with the distinction between **hazard, exposure, and risk**, because nearly every later concept depends on it. It then places WHMIS and Alberta occupational-health-and-safety requirements around that mental model without turning the course into a legal lecture. Learners next practise using labels, safety data sheets (SDSs), inventory information, task conditions, and the hierarchy of controls to make awareness-level decisions. The second half shifts from prevention to learning from failure: emergency response versus investigation, Alberta reporting boundaries, evidence collection, root-cause methods, human and organizational factors, CAPA, effectiveness checks, and reporting culture. [HC-WHMIS-RR; HPA-13; HPA-14; AB-OHS-2; AB-OHS-4; AB-OHS-7; AB-OHS-29; CCOHS-SDS; CCOHS-INVEST; HC-CAPA; CCOHS-INDICATORS]

The design deliberately distinguishes **law**, **government guidance**, **regulated-quality examples**, **international standards/guidance**, **peer-reviewed evidence**, and **reasoned good-practice synthesis**. It also preserves the research dossier’s safety boundary: learners will learn what to recognize, interpret, document, compare, and escalate, but the course will not authorize them to select respirators or gloves, set exposure limits, conduct industrial-hygiene sampling, perform specialized spill response, handle highly reactive chemicals without competent supervision, make legal reportability determinations, or carry out professional root-cause or process-safety analysis independently.

# 2. Audience, assumed prior knowledge, use context, and qualification boundaries

## 2.1 Intended audience

Primary learners are educated, nontechnical professionals working in or supporting:

- laboratory operations;
- biotechnology;
- healthcare or clinical laboratories;
- research programs;
- quality systems;
- environmental health and safety support;
- compliance;
- administration;
- project or program coordination;
- facilities or operational support where laboratory chemical risk is relevant.

The course is particularly appropriate for people who may need to participate in hazard reviews, maintain records, coordinate actions, support incident investigations, review CAPA progress, or work alongside technical specialists without themselves holding specialist credentials.

## 2.2 Assumed prior knowledge

Learners can be assumed to have:

- general workplace literacy;
- experience following organizational procedures;
- basic familiarity with the idea that chemicals can present hazards;
- basic ability to read tables, labels, short technical descriptions, and workplace records;
- no specialist knowledge of toxicology, chemistry, occupational hygiene, WHMIS law, incident analysis, CAPA, or quality systems.

The course should **not** assume that learners can already:

- distinguish hazard from risk;
- interpret WHMIS legal roles;
- understand the difference between a supplier label and a work-site label;
- read an SDS strategically;
- understand occupational exposure limits;
- distinguish an incident from a near miss or nonconformity;
- apply the hierarchy of controls;
- separate emergency response from investigation;
- distinguish correction, containment, corrective action, and preventive action;
- evaluate whether an action has actually been effective.

## 2.3 Likely use contexts

After the course, learners may use the concepts when:

- participating in a laboratory hazard assessment;
- reviewing a task before work starts;
- checking whether a change requires a risk review;
- maintaining chemical or SDS information;
- recognizing a labelling or storage issue;
- supporting laboratory inspections;
- escalating an exposure or uncontrolled event;
- participating in an incident review;
- helping collect records or evidence;
- contributing to a CAPA record;
- tracking corrective-action owners, due dates, and effectiveness checks;
- interpreting safety metrics;
- discussing recurring problems with technical or safety specialists.

## 2.4 Performance need

The course should move the learner from **passive awareness** to **structured participation**.

The learner should be able to:

- identify what information matters;
- distinguish legal requirements from recommendations;
- ask better questions;
- recognize when an apparent administrative issue has safety significance;
- identify when a task-specific risk assessment is needed;
- recognize when a control is lower or higher in the hierarchy;
- know when to stop and escalate;
- avoid treating an SDS as a complete safe-work procedure;
- avoid treating “worker error” as a complete investigation conclusion;
- distinguish immediate fixes from actions intended to prevent recurrence;
- recognize whether an effectiveness check actually tests the outcome.

## 2.5 Qualification boundaries

The course **does not qualify or authorize** learners to:

- act as an occupational hygienist;
- conduct professional exposure assessments or air-monitoring programs;
- select or approve respirators, cartridges, gloves, chemical suits, or other specialist PPE;
- establish occupational exposure limits;
- conduct medical evaluation;
- provide legal advice;
- decide legal reportability without the organization’s responsible function;
- perform hazardous chemical neutralization or spill response beyond their training;
- handle pyrophoric, highly reactive, peroxide-forming, or similarly high-hazard materials without competent supervision and local procedures;
- engineer ventilation systems or certify fume-hood performance;
- conduct reactive-chemistry scale-up;
- perform a professional fault-tree or process-safety analysis independently;
- claim WHMIS certification, occupational-health-and-safety certification, auditing certification, or CAPA certification.

These boundaries are necessary because the source dossier explicitly distinguishes awareness and decision support from professional, hazardous, legal, medical, engineering, or regulated work.

## 2.6 Key misconceptions and cognitive barriers

### Misconception 1: “Hazard” and “risk” mean the same thing

**Correction:** A hazard is something with the potential to cause harm. Risk depends on the conditions of the task, exposure opportunity, controls, and credible consequences. [CCOHS-SDS; AB-OHS-2]

### Misconception 2: “If we have the SDS, the task is assessed”

**Correction:** The SDS provides product-level hazard information and general precautions. It does not know the laboratory’s quantity, concentration, equipment, ventilation, duration, scale, process energy, incompatible materials, or abnormal conditions. [CCOHS-SDS]

### Misconception 3: “WHMIS is just labels and pictograms”

**Correction:** WHMIS includes classification, labels, SDSs, and worker education/training, and in Alberta it operates within a broader workplace hazard-assessment and control system. [HC-WHMIS-RR; AB-OHS-29]

### Misconception 4: “A small or temporary container does not need a label”

**Correction:** Alberta’s decanting exception has specific conditions. Laboratory and small-container rules are narrow variations, not blanket exemptions. [AB-OHS-29 s.400; AB-OHS-29 s.403; HPR-5; CCOHS-LABS]

### Misconception 5: “PPE is the main safety control”

**Correction:** Alberta’s hierarchy gives priority to elimination and engineering controls where reasonably practicable, followed by administrative controls and PPE as needed. [AB-OHS-2 s.9]

### Misconception 6: “No injury means nothing serious happened”

**Correction:** Alberta law requires investigation of some high-potential incidents even when serious injury did not occur, where the statutory test is met. [AB-OHS-ACT-33(5)]

### Misconception 7: “Worker error is the root cause”

**Correction:** CCOHS advises investigators to examine task, material, environment, personnel, and management factors and warns against stopping at “carelessness.” [CCOHS-INVEST]

### Misconception 8: “Retraining is always a corrective action”

**Correction:** Retraining may be appropriate when a competence failure is actually causal, but it does not address equipment, workflow, design, workload, maintenance, or management-system causes. [CCOHS-INVEST; HC-CAPA]

### Misconception 9: “CAPA is legally required in exactly the same way in every laboratory”

**Correction:** CAPA is a formal regulatory quality-system concept in some sectors. Health Canada’s NHP guidance is a regulated example, not a universal laboratory legal requirement. [HC-CAPA; HC-GMP-V4]

### Misconception 10: “A low incident count proves a safe laboratory”

**Correction:** Lagging indicators can be distorted by small numbers and underreporting. Leading indicators and reporting culture must also be considered. [CCOHS-INDICATORS; KYUNG-2023; PROBST-2008; LIPSCOMB-2013]

# 3. Recommended course duration and scope rationale

## 3.1 Recommended duration

**Recommended total self-paced duration: approximately 120 minutes, including formative practice and a graded capstone.**

Preferred delivery structure:

- **Part I — Chemical information, task risk, and controls:** approximately 58–62 minutes
- **Part II — Incident response, investigation, CAPA, and learning systems:** approximately 58–62 minutes

The two parts remain one course and should share the same objectives, glossary, source architecture, and final assessment.

## 3.2 Why two hours is appropriate

The evidence base contains two substantial performance domains:

1. **Prevention:** WHMIS, Alberta duties, labels, SDSs, inventory, task-specific risk, hierarchy of controls, storage, ventilation, change management.
2. **Response and learning:** emergency response, reporting boundaries, scene preservation, investigation, evidence, root-cause methods, human factors, CAPA, effectiveness, metrics.

Two hours permits learners to do more than recognize vocabulary. It provides enough space for:

- a usable hazard-to-risk mental model;
- one practical SDS/task interpretation exercise;
- one controls-selection exercise;
- one change/emergency decision exercise;
- one incident-analysis exercise;
- one CAPA/effectiveness exercise;
- a final integrated scenario.

The deeper legal tables, specialist exposure provisions, hazard-family detail, and source-maintenance material can remain optional or reference content without weakening the course.

## 3.3 Must know

The following should be available to every learner in the main path:

- hazard, exposure, risk, consequence, likelihood;
- incident, near miss, correction, containment, corrective action, preventive action, root cause, contributing factor, effectiveness check;
- WHMIS versus GHS;
- federal supplier versus Alberta workplace responsibility;
- labels, decanting, laboratory-sample limits at an awareness level;
- what an SDS can and cannot do;
- task-specific risk factors;
- hierarchy of controls;
- compatibility and storage principles;
- risk-assessment review triggers;
- emergency response versus investigation;
- escalation of serious/high-potential events;
- scene/evidence preservation concept;
- evidence categories;
- why “worker error” is not enough;
- correction versus CAPA;
- effectiveness checks;
- leading versus lagging indicators and reporting culture;
- qualification/escalation boundaries.

## 3.4 Useful to know

Provide through progressive disclosure or optional technical depth:

- HPR significant-new-data update timing;
- specific Alberta Part 4 provisions on combined exposure and long shifts;
- exact inventory field architecture;
- detailed comparison of RCA tools;
- details of Alberta investigation-report retention/distribution;
- specialized hazard-family conceptual controls;
- ISO 45001 status;
- WHO quality-system context;
- specific peer-reviewed reporting-culture studies.

## 3.5 Reference when needed

Make available in a reference layer or downloadable job aid:

- full 16-section SDS table;
- full source register;
- exact legislative section mapping;
- Alberta Schedule 1 / code-of-practice references;
- detailed hazard-family reference;
- inventory data-field checklist;
- investigation evidence checklist;
- CAPA record checklist;
- local-expert escalation list;
- current-edition/change-watch notes.

## 3.6 Excluded from learner instruction

Exclude from the instructional path:

- reaction recipes;
- neutralization procedures;
- universal spill quantities;
- universal glove selection;
- universal respirator/cartridge selection;
- chemical-specific exposure-limit tables;
- disposal instructions;
- pyrophoric/water-reactive operating procedures;
- peroxide-former testing procedures;
- medical advice;
- legal conclusions for specific incidents;
- engineering design instructions;
- inaccessible ISO clauses.

Reason: these would either exceed the evidence scope, create unsafe operationalization, imply specialist authority, or violate the source dossier’s explicit boundaries.

# 9. Detailed module plans

# Module 0 — Orientation and boundaries

## Instructional purpose

Establish why the course matters, what type of decisions learners will practise, and what the course does not authorize them to do.

## Prerequisite connection

No technical prerequisites. This module establishes the evidence hierarchy and prevents the learner from treating all later content as either “law” or “optional advice.”

## Content sequence

1. A laboratory chemical can have the same intrinsic hazard in two settings but different risk because the task conditions and controls differ.
2. The course uses Canadian WHMIS and Alberta OHS as its primary context.
3. Sources fall into different authority categories:
   - law/regulation;
   - government guidance;
   - international/management-system guidance;
   - peer-reviewed evidence;
   - good-practice synthesis.
4. Course performance boundary:
   - recognize;
   - interpret;
   - compare;
   - document;
   - escalate.
5. Explicit non-qualification boundary.

## Plain-language explanations required

**Law versus guidance:**  
“Law sets enforceable requirements. Guidance explains how a regulator or authoritative body interprets or supports those requirements. Good practice can still be useful, but it should not be described as law.”

**Awareness versus authorization:**  
“This course can help you recognize a problem and make better decisions about escalation. It does not qualify you to perform specialized hazardous work.”

## Misconceptions to correct

- “Anything in a safety course is legally mandatory.”
- “If I finish this course, I am qualified to select chemical PPE.”
- “A course can replace local procedures.”

## Proposed examples, visuals, and interactions

**Visual:** evidence ladder with five labelled levels.  
**Text equivalent:** ordered list describing the five source categories.

**Interaction:** three short statements labelled “law,” “government guidance,” or “good-practice synthesis.” The goal is source-type recognition, not memorization of statute numbers.

## Formative practice

One three-item classification activity with explanatory feedback.

## Citations

[HC-WHMIS-RR; HPA-13; HPA-14; AB-OHS-2; ISO-45001; WHO-LQMS; source dossier §26]

# Module 1 — From hazard to task-specific risk

## Instructional purpose

Give learners the mental model needed to interpret every later chemical-risk decision.

## Prerequisite connection

Builds directly from the orientation distinction between evidence and action.

## Content sequence

1. Define **hazard**.
2. Define **exposure** and common exposure routes.
3. Define **risk**.
4. Add **consequence** and **likelihood**.
5. Introduce the decision model:
   **hazard + task conditions + exposure opportunity + control performance + abnormal conditions → task risk**
6. Show why quantity, concentration, physical state, duration, frequency, proximity, process energy, and incompatibility matter.
7. Show why abnormal conditions belong in a risk assessment.
8. Introduce “residual risk” as a plain-language term for risk remaining after controls.
9. Emphasize that specialist judgment may be required for exposure monitoring, OEL interpretation, or unusual hazards.

## Plain-language explanations required

**Hazard:**  
“A hazard is something that can cause harm.”

**Exposure:**  
“Exposure happens when a person can actually come into contact with the hazard, for example by breathing it, getting it on the skin or eyes, swallowing contamination, or being injected through a sharp or pressurized system.”

**Risk:**  
“Risk is not a property printed on the bottle. It depends on what you are doing, how exposure could happen, what controls are in place, and what the consequences could be.”

**Consequence:**  
“How bad could the credible outcome be?”

**Likelihood:**  
“How likely is the event or exposure under the actual conditions?”

## Misconceptions to correct

- “Dangerous chemical = high risk in every use.”
- “Small amount = automatically low risk.”
- “Below an exposure limit means no further control is needed.”
- “If the normal procedure is safe, abnormal conditions do not need to be considered.”

## Proposed examples, visuals, and interactions

**Worked comparison:** same corrosive product in:
- sealed storage;
- small controlled transfer;
- larger heated/open process.

No quantitative risk score is needed. Learners identify which task variables change.

**Visual:** hazard-to-risk funnel or flow diagram.  
**Text equivalent:** numbered decision sequence.

**Interaction:** identify exposure routes and risk-changing variables from a short scenario.

## Formative practice

Two short activities:
1. classify statements as hazard, exposure, consequence, or likelihood;
2. select which task details are required before judging risk.

## Citations

[AB-OHS-2; AB-OHS-4 ss.16, 21; CCOHS-SDS]

# Module 2 — WHMIS, labels, SDSs, and inventory

## Instructional purpose

Teach learners where chemical hazard information comes from, how it moves through the workplace, and how to use it without over-relying on it.

## Prerequisite connection

Learners now understand that product hazard information is only one input into task risk.

## Content sequence

1. WHMIS purpose and four elements:
   - classification;
   - labels;
   - SDSs;
   - worker education/training. [HC-WHMIS-RR]
2. WHMIS versus GHS:
   - GHS is the international model;
   - Canadian law determines Canadian requirements. [UNECE-GHS11; HC-WHMIS-GUIDE]
3. Responsibility split:
   - supplier classification/communication under HPA/HPR;
   - employer workplace training, labelling, SDS access, hazard management under Alberta law. [HPA-13; HPA-14; AB-OHS-29]
4. Supplier label versus work-site label.
5. Decanted products:
   - Alberta immediate-use exception has specific conditions. [AB-OHS-29 s.400]
6. Laboratory samples:
   - not every “sample in a lab” qualifies;
   - HPR laboratory-sample definition is narrow. [HPR-5]
7. Small containers as enrichment, not a memorization target. [CCOHS-LABS]
8. SDS 16-section architecture.
9. Strategic reading:
   - Section 1 identity;
   - Section 2 hazards;
   - Section 3 composition;
   - Sections 7/10 handling/reactivity;
   - Section 8 controls;
   - Section 9 physical properties;
   - Section 11 health effects;
   - Sections 4–6 emergency information. [CCOHS-SDS]
10. SDS limitation:
    - cannot substitute for task-specific risk assessment.
11. Inventory as risk infrastructure:
    - identity;
    - ownership;
    - quantity;
    - location;
    - storage class;
    - SDS link;
    - lifecycle/status. [CCOHS-LABS + synthesis]

## Plain-language explanations required

**WHMIS versus GHS:**  
“GHS is the international model. WHMIS is Canada’s legal workplace system built using GHS concepts.”

**Work-site label:**  
“A work-site label keeps key identity and safe-handling information with the product when the original supplier label is not the label being used.”

**SDS:**  
“An SDS tells you important things about the product. It does not know your exact task.”

**Inventory:**  
“A good inventory is not just a list of bottles. It tells the organization what it has, where it is, who owns it, what information applies, and where it is in its lifecycle.”

## Misconceptions to correct

- “WHMIS is only pictograms.”
- “GHS automatically equals Canadian legal compliance.”
- “Anything decanted for a short time is exempt from a work-site label.”
- “All lab samples are exempt.”
- “The SDS gives the complete answer for safe handling.”
- “Inventory is clerical housekeeping.”

## Proposed examples, visuals, and interactions

**Visual:** split-responsibility diagram:
- supplier;
- employer/workplace;
- worker.

**Visual:** SDS map showing high-use sections by task stage.  
**Text equivalent:** ordered list.

**Interaction 1:** determine whether a portable-container situation clearly fits the immediate-use conditions or should be escalated for work-site labelling. Avoid edge-case legal puzzles.

**Interaction 2:** “Where would you look?” Match common questions to SDS sections.

**Interaction 3:** identify which inventory fields are operationally important in a simple scenario.

## Formative practice

One integrated scenario:
- a worker transfers a hazardous product;
- learner identifies label issue;
- learner selects useful SDS sections;
- learner identifies missing task-risk information.

## Citations

[HC-WHMIS-RR; HPA-13; HPA-14; HPR; HPR-5; HC-WHMIS-GUIDE; AB-OHS-29 ss.397–407; CCOHS-LABS; CCOHS-SDS; UNECE-GHS11]

# Module 3 — Controls, storage, ventilation, and change

## Instructional purpose

Teach learners to move from identified risk to better control decisions.

## Prerequisite connection

Uses the task-risk model and chemical information from Modules 1–2.

## Content sequence

1. Alberta hazard-assessment and control sequence. [AB-OHS-2 ss.7–9]
2. Hierarchy:
   - elimination;
   - substitution;
   - engineering;
   - administrative;
   - PPE.
3. Why higher-level controls are generally preferred.
4. Why PPE is necessary but limited.
5. Compatibility-based storage:
   - do not rely on alphabetical arrangement or single pictogram;
   - use actual compatibility information. [CCOHS-CABINETS]
6. Secondary containment as a compatibility/control concept.
7. Hazard-family overview:
   - flammables;
   - oxidizers;
   - corrosives;
   - acute toxins;
   - sensitizers;
   - carcinogens/reproductive hazards;
   - gases;
   - cryogens;
   - peroxide-formers;
   - pyrophoric/water-reactive substances;
   - hazardous waste.
8. For high-hazard families, teach recognition/escalation, not handling procedures.
9. Local exhaust and fume-hood principles. [CCOHS-VENT; CCOHS-HOODS]
10. Change management:
    - legal triggers: new process, changed operation, significant work-site alteration, periodic review. [AB-OHS-2 s.7(4)]
    - additional good-practice triggers: new material, scale-up, equipment/ventilation changes, incident, new evidence, personnel/competency changes.
11. Specialist boundaries:
    - exposure monitoring;
    - respiratory protection;
    - high-hazard process engineering;
    - unusual compatibility.

## Plain-language explanations required

**Hierarchy:**  
“The best control is usually the one that removes or isolates the hazard before relying on a person to remember the right action every time.”

**Engineering control:**  
“A physical or designed control that changes the workplace or equipment so exposure is less likely.”

**Administrative control:**  
“A rule, procedure, schedule, training requirement, or work practice.”

**PPE:**  
“PPE protects the individual wearer and depends on correct selection and use. It does not remove the hazard.”

**Compatibility:**  
“Chemicals should be stored according to how they can react with each other, not simply by alphabet or one hazard symbol.”

## Misconceptions to correct

- “PPE is the strongest control.”
- “Training fixes any safety problem.”
- “All acids belong together.”
- “A fume hood is automatically suitable for any chemical task.”
- “Risk assessment is done once and stays valid forever.”

## Proposed examples, visuals, and interactions

**Visual:** hierarchy pyramid or vertical ladder with equal text labels and a text equivalent.

**Interaction:** five proposed actions for a recurring splash problem. Learner ranks:
1. eliminate unnecessary open transfer;
2. enclose/automate;
3. revise procedure;
4. retrain;
5. add PPE.

The feedback should explain that actual selection depends on feasibility and task details, but higher-order controls deserve first consideration. [AB-OHS-2 s.9]

**Scenario:** a process moves from small room-temperature handling to larger heated volume. Learner identifies why a reassessment is required.

**Optional enrichment cards:** one per hazard family, each limited to:
- characteristic risk;
- conceptual control;
- escalation boundary.

## Formative practice

A control-selection scenario plus a change-trigger scenario.

## Citations

[AB-OHS-2 ss.7–10; AB-OHS-4 ss.16, 21, 23–27; CCOHS-CABINETS; CCOHS-FLAME; CCOHS-OX; CCOHS-VENT; CCOHS-HOODS]

# Module 4 — When something goes wrong: response, reporting, and preservation

## Instructional purpose

Teach the learner to distinguish immediate emergency priorities from later investigation and to recognize when escalation is required.

## Prerequisite connection

Builds on abnormal-condition thinking from Module 1 and change/control concepts from Module 3.

## Content sequence

1. Immediate response principles:
   - protect life;
   - warn others;
   - stop/evacuate where required;
   - activate local emergency process;
   - obtain medical/emergency assistance;
   - isolate within training and authority;
   - prevent escalation.
2. Why there is no universal spill-cleanup quantity.
3. Alberta Part 7 emergency planning. [AB-OHS-7]
4. Serious/reportable event awareness under s.33. [AB-OHS-ACT-33]
5. High-potential incident investigation trigger. [AB-OHS-ACT-33(5)]
6. Internal “near miss” label does not determine statutory duties.
7. Scene preservation for reportable events:
   - preserve after safety;
   - understand statutory exceptions. [AB-OHS-ACT-33(9)]
8. Investigation report/retention details as optional technical depth. [AB-OHS-ACT-33(6)–(7)]
9. Learner responsibility:
   - recognize;
   - stop;
   - report/escalate;
   - preserve information when directed;
   - do not independently make legal determinations beyond role.

## Plain-language explanations required

**Emergency versus investigation:**  
“During the emergency, protect people and stop the situation from getting worse. Investigation starts after the immediate danger is controlled.”

**High-potential incident:**  
“An event can matter legally even if nobody was seriously hurt. Alberta requires investigation of some incidents that had a likelihood of causing serious injury or illness where corrective action may be needed.” [AB-OHS-ACT-33(5)]

**Scene preservation:**  
“Do not disturb the scene of a reportable event just to make it look normal. Safety and care come first, and the law provides specific exceptions.” [AB-OHS-ACT-33(9)]

## Misconceptions to correct

- “We should photograph everything before helping the injured worker.”
- “No injury means no investigation.”
- “Near miss is only an internal administrative term with no legal significance.”
- “I should personally decide whether the regulator must be called.”
- “Every spill under a certain volume is safe to clean.”

## Proposed examples, visuals, and interactions

**Visual:** two-phase timeline:
1. emergency control;
2. investigation and learning.

**Decision interaction:** four events sorted into:
- routine issue / local process;
- immediate emergency escalation;
- high-potential investigation consideration;
- serious/reportable event escalation.

Avoid asking learners to make final legal determinations in ambiguous cases.

## Formative practice

Branching decision:
- uncontrolled reaction;
- no injury yet;
- learner chooses immediate priority;
- then chooses investigation/escalation action.

[AB-OHS-7 ss.115–118; AB-OHS-ACT-33]

# Module 5 — Investigating causes, not blame

## Instructional purpose

Give non-investigators a disciplined way to support evidence collection and causal analysis without overclaiming expertise.

## Prerequisite connection

Starts after the Module 4 distinction that investigation follows emergency stabilization.

## Content sequence

1. Investigation purpose:
   - learn what happened;
   - understand why;
   - prevent recurrence.
2. Evidence categories:
   - physical;
   - documentary;
   - electronic/system;
   - witness.
3. Preserve facts before interpretation.
4. Witness interviewing:
   - open questions;
   - normal versus event conditions;
   - distinguish observation from inference;
   - avoid blame-laden language;
   - respect immediate trauma/medical needs.
5. Timeline analysis.
6. 5 Whys:
   - useful for pushing beyond immediate explanation;
   - weak if forced into one linear chain. [CCOHS-INVEST]
7. Fishbone as optional broadening tool.
8. Barrier analysis:
   - what should have prevented/detected/mitigated the event?
9. Change analysis:
   - what was different from normal?
10. Fault-tree analysis as reference only.
11. System-factor framework:
   - task;
   - equipment;
   - materials;
   - environment;
   - workload;
   - training/competence;
   - supervision;
   - procurement;
   - maintenance;
   - management systems.
12. Why “worker error” is not a sufficient endpoint. [CCOHS-INVEST]
13. Differentiate:
   - direct event;
   - contributing factor;
   - plausible hypothesis;
   - evidenced cause.

## Plain-language explanations required

**Evidence versus explanation:**  
“Evidence is what you can observe, document, measure, or reliably establish. A cause is an explanation supported by that evidence.”

**Worker error:**  
“Saying a person made a mistake describes part of the event. It does not tell you why the system allowed the mistake to lead to harm.”

**Barrier analysis:**  
“Ask what should have stopped the event, whether the barrier existed, and why it failed or was missing.”

**Change analysis:**  
“Compare the event with how the work normally happens. What changed?”

## Misconceptions to correct

- “The first plausible cause is the root cause.”
- “5 Whys proves causation.”
- “A witness’s interpretation is the same as direct observation.”
- “If the procedure was written correctly, the system worked.”
- “Retraining automatically follows worker error.”

## Proposed examples, visuals, and interactions

**Visual:** evidence wheel with four categories and text equivalent.

**Worked example:** waste near miss:
- ambiguous labels;
- shared waste station;
- shorthand naming;
- worker stops on noticing heat.

Learner builds a simple timeline and identifies missing evidence before causal conclusions.

**Interaction:** convert blame statements into investigation questions:
- “The worker was careless” → “What task, label, equipment, workload, or supervision conditions shaped the action?”

**Optional interaction:** choose best method:
- timeline;
- barrier analysis;
- change analysis;
- 5 Whys.

## Formative practice

Mini evidence packet:
- short procedure excerpt;
- two witness statements;
- inventory/waste label note;
- prior inspection note.

Learner selects:
- facts;
- questions;
- plausible contributing factors;
- unsupported assumptions.

# Module 6 — CAPA: from cause to effective action

## Instructional purpose

Teach learners to construct a defensible link from problem to cause to action to effectiveness.

## Prerequisite connection

Requires Module 5’s distinction between evidence and cause.

## Content sequence

1. CAPA applicability boundary:
   - formal in some regulated quality systems;
   - transferable as management practice;
   - not a universal legal requirement for every lab. [HC-CAPA; HC-GMP-V4]
2. Define:
   - correction;
   - containment;
   - corrective action;
   - preventive action.
3. CAPA record structure:
   - problem statement;
   - scope;
   - risk/impact;
   - immediate action;
   - evidence;
   - causal analysis;
   - action;
   - owner;
   - resources;
   - due date;
   - interim controls;
   - change/document updates;
   - verification;
   - effectiveness;
   - closure/escalation. [HC-CAPA]
4. Link each action to a causal factor.
5. Apply hierarchy of controls to CAPA selection.
6. Why vague commitments or retraining-only responses may be weak.
7. Verification versus effectiveness.
8. Design an effectiveness check:
   - intended outcome;
   - measure;
   - timing/opportunities;
   - threshold;
   - reviewer;
   - response if ineffective.
9. Reopen/escalate when ineffective.
10. Distinguish CAPA closure from “all tasks marked complete.”

## Plain-language explanations required

**Correction:**  
“Fix the problem you can see now.”

**Containment:**  
“Keep the problem from spreading or causing more harm while you investigate.”

**Corrective action:**  
“Address why the detected problem happened.”

**Preventive action:**  
“Address the same or a similar failure before it happens elsewhere.”

**Verification:**  
“Did we complete the action?”

**Effectiveness:**  
“Did the action actually reduce or remove the problem?”

## Misconceptions to correct

- “Correction and corrective action are the same.”
- “CAPA means retraining.”
- “A completed action is an effective action.”
- “One root cause must always exist.”
- “Every research laboratory is legally required to use the same CAPA model.”

## Proposed examples, visuals, and interactions

**Visual:** CAPA logic chain:
problem → immediate control → evidence → cause → action → verification → effectiveness → closure/feedback.

**Worked example:** recurring inventory failures.
- correction: fix records;
- containment: restrict uncertain materials;
- corrective action: redesign lifecycle;
- preventive action: apply the workflow to other labs;
- effectiveness: physical/database match after defined review cycles.

**Interaction:** classify eight actions as correction, containment, corrective, preventive, or “not enough information.”

**Interaction:** compare three action packages:
- retraining only;
- engineering/process redesign plus training;
- paperwork only.

Learner identifies which is best aligned to the stated cause.

## Formative practice

One CAPA logic-chain activity plus one effectiveness-check selection.

## Citations

[HC-CAPA; HC-GMP-V4; CCOHS-INVEST; AB-OHS-2 s.9]

# Module 7 — Metrics, reporting culture, and continual improvement

## Instructional purpose

Prevent learners from treating low event counts as proof of a healthy system and connect measurement to the earlier control/CAPA cycle.

## Prerequisite connection

Builds on effectiveness checks and recurring-problem recognition.

## Content sequence

1. Leading indicators:
   - risk assessments reviewed;
   - controls verified;
   - overdue actions;
   - inspection completion/closure;
   - near-miss investigations;
   - effectiveness checks. [CCOHS-INDICATORS]
2. Lagging indicators:
   - injuries;
   - exposures;
   - spills;
   - fires;
   - reportable incidents;
   - repeat nonconformities.
3. Why both are needed.
4. Why incident counts can mislead.
5. Underreporting factors:
   - fear;
   - cumbersome process;
   - poor knowledge;
   - distrust;
   - weak safety climate. [KYUNG-2023]
6. Evidence caveat:
   - peer-reviewed studies cited are not laboratory-specific prevalence estimates.
7. Metric design:
   - do not reward silence;
   - reward useful reporting and timely response;
   - evaluate whether reports lead to visible action.

## Plain-language explanations required

**Leading indicator:**  
“A measure of conditions or activities intended to prevent problems before harm occurs.”

**Lagging indicator:**  
“A measure of outcomes after something has already happened.”

**Reporting culture:**  
“A strong reporting system makes it safe and worthwhile to report problems and then acts on what is reported.”

## Misconceptions to correct

- “More near-miss reports means safety is getting worse.”
- “Zero incidents proves zero risk.”
- “Closing more CAPAs always means improvement.”

## Proposed examples, visuals, and interactions

**Comparison table:** leading versus lagging examples with text equivalent.

**Scenario:** Lab A has few reports but many overdue inspections; Lab B has more near-miss reports and closes high-risk findings quickly. Learner identifies why incident count alone is insufficient. Do not ask which lab is “better” overall; ask which additional information is needed to judge system health.

## Formative practice

One indicator-classification activity plus one reporting-culture interpretation item.

## Citations

[CCOHS-INDICATORS; KYUNG-2023; PROBST-2008; LIPSCOMB-2013]

# Module 8 — Integrated capstone and transfer

## Instructional purpose

Require the learner to combine prevention, escalation, investigation, and CAPA reasoning in one realistic laboratory case.

## Scenario structure

Use the recurring waste-interface case as the main capstone because it can touch nearly every objective without requiring unsafe chemical detail.

### Stage 1 — Before the event
Learner sees:
- shared waste area;
- project abbreviations;
- incomplete inventory/waste information;
- a recent change in one process.

Ask:
- what hazard/risk information is missing?
- what should trigger review?
- what control categories deserve attention?

### Stage 2 — Near miss
Worker begins using the wrong waste interface, notices unexpected heat, and stops.

Ask:
- what is the first priority?
- what should be escalated?
- why is “nobody was hurt” not the end of the matter?

### Stage 3 — Investigation
Provide:
- label photo description;
- SOP excerpt;
- witness statement;
- prior inspection note.

Ask:
- what is fact?
- what is interpretation?
- what evidence is still needed?
- which system factors should be examined?

### Stage 4 — CAPA
Offer actions:
- retrain the worker;
- standardize waste identification;
- physically separate incompatible streams;
- revise the procedure;
- audit effectiveness.

Ask:
- which are correction, containment, corrective, or preventive?
- which higher-order actions best align with identified causes?
- what effectiveness check would show whether risk was actually reduced?

## Transfer-to-work close

Learners leave with a seven-question mental checklist:

1. **What is the hazard?**
2. **How could exposure or an uncontrolled event occur in this task?**
3. **What information am I using, and what information is missing?**
4. **Are higher-order controls possible?**
5. **Has anything changed that requires reassessment?**
6. **If something went wrong, is the first need emergency control, escalation, investigation, or all three in sequence?**
7. **Does the corrective action address the cause, and how will effectiveness be checked?**

[AB-OHS-2; AB-OHS-7; AB-OHS-ACT-33; AB-OHS-29; CCOHS-SDS; CCOHS-INVEST; HC-CAPA]

The final integrated scenario should contribute a meaningful portion of the score because it tests combined reasoning. Avoid making the assessment mostly vocabulary recall.

# 12. Proposed glossary and acronym list

## Core glossary

**Administrative control**  
A rule, procedure, schedule, training requirement, access restriction, or other work-practice control. [AB-OHS-2 s.9]

**ALARA**  
“As low as reasonably achievable.” In Alberta Part 4, exposure to harmful substances is subject to this principle in addition to applicable exposure-limit requirements. [AB-OHS-4 s.16]

**CAPA**  
Corrective and preventive action. A formal quality-system concept in some regulated contexts and a useful management framework more broadly. It is not automatically a universal legal requirement for every laboratory. [HC-CAPA; HC-GMP-V4]

**Containment**  
Immediate or interim action used to limit spread, exposure, or impact while the problem is investigated. [HC-CAPA]

**Consequence / severity**  
The credible magnitude of harm if an event or exposure occurs.

**Contributing factor**  
A condition or action that increased the likelihood or severity of an event but may not explain the event by itself. [CCOHS-INVEST]

**Correction**  
Action that fixes the immediate detected problem. [HC-CAPA]

**Corrective action**  
Action intended to address a cause of a detected problem so recurrence is less likely. [HC-CAPA]

**Deviation**  
A departure from an approved or expected process, procedure, method, condition, or requirement. Exact definitions vary by quality system.

**Effectiveness check**  
A planned review that asks whether an implemented action actually produced the intended improvement. [HC-CAPA]

**Engineering control**  
A physical or designed control that removes, isolates, or reduces exposure to a hazard. [AB-OHS-2 s.9]

**Exposure**  
Contact between a person and a harmful agent through a relevant route such as inhalation, skin, eye, ingestion, or injection. [AB-OHS-4]

**GHS**  
Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals, the UN international model used as a basis for hazard classification and communication systems. [UNECE-GHS11]

**Hazard**  
A source, material, situation, or condition with the potential to cause harm. [AB-OHS-2]

**Hierarchy of controls**  
A structured approach that prioritizes elimination and higher-order controls before relying on administrative controls and PPE. Alberta’s specific legal sequence is set out in OHS Code s.9. [AB-OHS-2 s.9]

**Incident**  
An undesired event that caused or could have caused harm or loss. Organizations may define internal reporting categories differently. [CCOHS-INVEST]

**Likelihood**  
The chance that an event or exposure will occur under the defined conditions.

**Near miss**  
An event in which serious harm did not occur but could credibly have occurred. Internal terminology does not replace Alberta’s statutory tests for investigation/reporting. [AB-OHS-ACT-33]

**Nonconformity**  
Failure to meet a requirement.

**Occupational exposure limit (OEL)**  
A regulated benchmark used to control occupational exposure to airborne substances. Applying specific limits may require competent occupational-hygiene expertise. [AB-OHS-4 ss.16–20]

**Personal protective equipment (PPE)**  
Equipment worn by an individual to reduce exposure or injury. PPE is lower in the hierarchy than elimination/engineering where higher-order controls are reasonably practicable. [AB-OHS-2 s.9]

**Preventive action**  
Action intended to prevent a potential or related problem before recurrence or occurrence elsewhere. Terminology varies by management system. [HC-CAPA]

**Residual risk**  
Plain-language design term for risk remaining after existing controls are considered. This is not presented as a statutory Alberta definition.

**Risk**  
A judgment about the likelihood and consequence of harm under specific task and control conditions. [AB-OHS-2]

**Root cause**  
An underlying causal factor whose effective control should materially reduce recurrence. Complex incidents can have multiple causes. [CCOHS-INVEST]

**Safety Data Sheet (SDS)**  
A standardized hazardous-product information document. It supports hazard communication but is not a complete task-specific risk assessment. [CCOHS-SDS]

**Substitution**  
Replacing a hazard or process with a less hazardous alternative, while reassessing the resulting risk.

**WHMIS**  
Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System, Canada’s workplace hazard-communication system. [HC-WHMIS-RR]

**Work-site label**  
The Alberta workplace label used in specified circumstances for hazardous products, including many decanted products. [AB-OHS-29]

## Acronyms

- **ALARA** — As Low As Reasonably Achievable
- **CAPA** — Corrective and Preventive Action
- **CCOHS** — Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety
- **GHS** — Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals
- **HPA** — Hazardous Products Act
- **HPR** — Hazardous Products Regulations
- **JHSC** — Joint Health and Safety Committee
- **LEV** — Local Exhaust Ventilation
- **OEL** — Occupational Exposure Limit
- **OHS** — Occupational Health and Safety
- **PPE** — Personal Protective Equipment
- **RCA** — Root-Cause Analysis
- **SDS** — Safety Data Sheet
- **WHMIS** — Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System
