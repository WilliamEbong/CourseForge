# Course 6 — Laboratory Chemical Risk, Incident Investigation, and CAPA

```text
Conduct a rigorous, source-based research project for a portfolio course titled:

“Laboratory Chemical Risk, Incident Investigation, and CAPA”

PURPOSE AND AUDIENCE

Create a comprehensive research dossier that will later become an instructional course for educated, nontechnical professionals working in laboratory operations, biotechnology, healthcare, research, quality, safety, compliance, administration, or project coordination.

The eventual learners should not be assumed to be chemists, occupational hygienists, investigators, or quality professionals. The final course will use approximately a high-school reading level, but this research dossier should preserve necessary technical and regulatory precision.

This is an independent instructional-design sample. It must not imply occupational-health, WHMIS, quality, auditing, or CAPA certification. Do not create the course or storyboard yet.

GEOGRAPHIC AND REGULATORY EMPHASIS

Use Canadian WHMIS requirements and Alberta occupational-health-and-safety requirements as the primary context. Explain the division of responsibility among federal supplier requirements, provincial workplace requirements, employers, supervisors, and workers.

Verify the latest current consolidated legislation, current editions, effective dates, and guidance on the date you conduct the research. Clearly distinguish:

- Legislation and enforceable requirements
- Government guidance
- Management-system standards
- Laboratory quality practices
- Organization-specific procedures

The document must explain principles and decision processes, not provide jurisdiction-specific legal advice.

REQUIRED RESEARCH QUESTIONS

1. Define hazard, exposure, risk, consequence, likelihood, incident, near miss, nonconformity, deviation, correction, containment, corrective action, preventive action, root cause, contributing factor, and effectiveness check.
2. How do WHMIS and the UN Globally Harmonized System relate? What are the roles of classification, labels, safety data sheets, and worker education?
3. What WHMIS provisions and variations are particularly relevant to laboratories, including decanted products, laboratory samples, research samples, workplace labels, and small quantities?
4. What does Alberta’s OHS framework require regarding hazard assessment, elimination and control, chemical and biological hazards, harmful substances, exposure limits, emergency preparedness, worker participation, training, and incident reporting?
5. How should a laboratory establish and maintain a chemical inventory? Examine ownership, identifiers, quantity, location, storage class, SDS linkage, review dates, expiry or stability concerns, restricted access, and disposal status.
6. How should personnel read and critically use an SDS? Explain its 16-section structure and its limits—for example, why possession of an SDS is not itself a task-specific risk assessment.
7. How are chemical hazards translated into task-specific risks? Consider route and duration of exposure, concentration, quantity, volatility, dust or aerosol generation, pressure, temperature, reactivity, incompatibility, frequency, worker characteristics, equipment, and emergency conditions.
8. How is the hierarchy of controls applied in laboratories? Compare elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and PPE, including the limitations of PPE.
9. What conceptual controls apply to common laboratory hazard groups such as flammables, oxidizers, corrosives, acutely toxic chemicals, sensitizers, carcinogens, reproductive hazards, compressed gases, cryogens, peroxide-formers, pyrophoric or water-reactive substances, and hazardous waste?
10. How should laboratories address compatibility-based storage, segregation, secondary containment, ventilation, fume-hood use, access control, inspections, housekeeping, transport within a facility, and waste interfaces?
11. When must a risk assessment be reviewed—for example, after a process change, new material, scale-up, equipment change, facility change, incident, new evidence, or personnel change?
12. What should happen immediately after a chemical spill, exposure, equipment failure, injury, near miss, or uncontrolled reaction? Distinguish emergency response from later investigation.
13. How should an incident scene and evidence be protected while ensuring safety and required reporting?
14. How should an investigation team collect physical, documentary, system, and witness evidence? Address trauma-informed and blame-aware interviewing.
15. Compare appropriate uses and limitations of investigation methods such as timelines, 5 Whys, fishbone diagrams, barrier analysis, change analysis, and fault-tree analysis.
16. How can investigators avoid stopping at “worker error” and instead examine task design, equipment, materials, environment, workload, training, supervision, procurement, maintenance, and management systems?
17. How do immediate correction, containment, corrective action, and preventive action differ?
18. What constitutes a credible CAPA record? Research problem definition, scope, risk rating, root-cause evidence, actions, owners, resources, due dates, interim controls, change control, documentation, verification, effectiveness review, closure, and escalation.
19. How should corrective actions be prioritized using the hierarchy of controls and risk, rather than defaulting to retraining?
20. What indicators can show whether a safety and CAPA system is functioning? Distinguish useful leading and lagging indicators and discuss the risk of metrics that suppress reporting.
21. Develop two or three realistic laboratory cases for later instruction: one chemical near miss, one reportable or serious incident, and one recurring quality or safety nonconformity.

SAFETY AND SCOPE EXCLUSIONS

Do not provide reaction recipes, synthesis procedures, instructions for handling highly hazardous materials without competent supervision, or universal spill-response quantities. Do not recommend respirators, gloves, cartridges, exposure limits, disposal routes, or emergency actions without explaining that selection depends on the substance, task, SDS, local risk assessment, and qualified institutional personnel.

Do not treat CAPA as a universal legal requirement in every laboratory context. Distinguish occupational-safety incident management from regulated pharmaceutical, diagnostic, or manufacturing quality systems. Do not turn the document into legal, medical, emergency-response, or professional industrial-hygiene advice.

CITATION AND EVIDENCE RULES

- Cite every important factual, legal, technical, or normative claim inline.
- Link directly to the authoritative document.
- For legislation and long guidance, include the applicable part, section, article, requirement, or page when possible.
- Never invent citations, quotations, clauses, dates, or page numbers.
- State the jurisdiction and applicability of every legal requirement.
- Clearly label recommendations that are synthesis or good practice rather than law.
- Where sources use CAPA terminology differently, explain the difference.
- Record source title, issuing organization, edition/version, publication or update date, URL, and access date.
- Prefer official Canadian sources, regulators, standards bodies, WHO, and peer-reviewed research. Avoid vendor blogs and generic safety-training websites.
- Include a claim-to-source table so citations survive later instructional-design revisions.

1. Title, date, jurisdiction, intended later audience, and scope
2. Executive summary
3. Terminology and conceptual model
4. Canadian WHMIS and Alberta OHS framework
5. Chemical information, inventory, labels, and SDSs
6. Hazard identification and task-specific risk assessment
7. Control selection, laboratory operations, and change management
8. Emergency actions and reporting boundaries

10. Root-cause analysis and human/organizational factors
11. CAPA development, implementation, effectiveness, and closure
12. Performance measurement, reporting culture, and continual improvement
13. Two or three applied cases with evidence-based analysis
14. Matters requiring local expert or emergency judgment
15. Evidence gaps, conflicts, and changing requirements
16. Claim-to-source table
17. Annotated bibliography grouped by legislation, Canadian guidance, international standards/guidance, and peer-reviewed evidence

Aim for approximately 4,000–7,000 words, excluding the bibliography. Accuracy and traceability are more important than length.
```
