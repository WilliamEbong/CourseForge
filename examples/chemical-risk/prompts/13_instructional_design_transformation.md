# Shared Prompt 13 Instructional Design Transformation

Paste this prompt into each course chat after its cited research dossier is complete.

```text
Use the complete research dossier already produced in this conversation to create an instructional-design blueprint for the same course. This is the second stage of the workflow. Do not create the final course storyboard yet.

PRIMARY REQUIREMENT

Reorganize the researched material into the sequence and structure best suited for learning without changing the meaning of the evidence, weakening important qualifications, or separating factual claims from their citations.

The research dossier remains the factual source of truth. Preserve its source identifiers, inline citations, URLs, jurisdictional limits, dates, uncertainty, and distinctions among law, regulatory guidance, standards, empirical evidence, practitioner guidance, vendor documentation, and reasoned synthesis.

AUDIENCE AND TECHNICAL LEVEL

Design for educated, nontechnical professionals. Assume general workplace literacy but no specialist qualification in the course domain. Write proposed learner-facing explanations at approximately a high-school reading level, generally Grade 9 to Grade 11.

This does not mean removing necessary technical content. Instead:

- introduce concepts before relying on them;
- define technical terms on first use;
- explain acronyms before using them;
- use plain language and concrete examples;
- break complex systems into meaningful steps;
- preserve scientific, regulatory, ethical, and operational accuracy;
- distinguish an accessible overview from professional qualification or authorization to perform regulated work.

NON-NEGOTIABLE CONTENT AND CITATION RULES

1. Do not invent facts, examples presented as facts, statistics, requirements, quotations, standards clauses, incidents, or citations.
2. Do not silently omit researched content. Place each substantive item in one of four categories:
   - core course content;
   - optional enrichment or technical detail;
   - glossary/reference material;
   - excluded material, with a specific reason such as duplication, being outside scope, being unsafe to operationalize, or being unsupported.
3. Preserve citations immediately beside the claim or content point they support. If several source claims are combined, retain every citation needed to support the combined statement.
4. Preserve material qualifications, exceptions, evidence limitations, jurisdiction, dates, versions, and uncertainty.
5. Do not convert recommendations or emerging evidence into universal requirements.
6. Do not treat voluntary standards, models, or frameworks as law.
7. Do not turn awareness-level content into instructions that authorize learners to perform specialized, hazardous, clinical, engineering, legal, or regulated work.
8. You may conduct targeted additional research only when a genuine prerequisite or contextual gap would prevent comprehension. For every added fact, use the same source-quality and citation rules as the original research. Mark it as “gap-filling research” in the gap log. If a gap cannot be filled reliably, identify it instead of guessing.
9. Do not replace authoritative sources with easier secondary summaries merely to simplify the language. Simplify the explanation while retaining the authoritative citation.
10. Do not begin writing final screens, slides, or assessment questions during this stage.

INSTRUCTIONAL-DESIGN TASKS

A. Analyze the audience and performance need

- Describe what the learners are likely to know already.
- Identify what they need to understand, recognize, compare, decide, document, or escalate after the course.
- State what the course will not qualify them to do.
- Identify misconceptions, cognitive barriers, and unfamiliar prerequisite concepts.

B. Establish an appropriate scope and duration

- Recommend a realistic self-paced course duration based on the evidence and intended outcomes.
- Prefer a focused course of roughly 60 to 90 minutes where the content supports it.
- If the material cannot be taught responsibly in that range, recommend a longer course or a clearly defined course series rather than deleting essential content.
- Separate “must know,” “useful to know,” and “reference when needed” material.

C. Create measurable learning objectives

- Write four to six measurable terminal learning objectives.
- Use observable verbs suited to the audience, such as explain, distinguish, identify, interpret, compare, select, evaluate, document, or escalate.
- Avoid vague verbs such as know, understand, learn, or appreciate unless paired with observable performance.
- Ensure every objective is supported by the cited research and can be assessed at the intended level.
- Do not imply certification-level competence.

D. Sequence and scaffold the content

Use a progression such as:

1. relevance and purpose;
2. prerequisite concepts and vocabulary;
3. a clear mental model or process overview;
4. components, stages, methods, or options;
5. benefits, limitations, risks, and evidence quality;
6. applied decisions or scenarios;
7. review and transfer to the learner's work.

Adapt this sequence when the subject requires a different learning logic. Explain the reason for major sequencing decisions.

E. Design for comprehension and retention

- Chunk the content around one meaningful idea or decision at a time.
- Use progressive disclosure: essential explanation first, optional technical depth second.
- Identify where a diagram, process map, comparison table, timeline, worked example, decision tree, or scenario would materially improve comprehension.
- Use analogies only when they are accurate, bounded, and followed by the literal explanation.
- Build retrieval practice and spaced review into the sequence.
- Include realistic, neutral scenarios, but do not invent real organizations, outcomes, or quantitative results.

F. Plan practice and assessment alignment

- Map every learning objective to its supporting content, at least one formative practice opportunity, and an appropriate graded-assessment method.
- Recommend a balanced set of interactions such as comparison, categorization, sequencing, matching, data interpretation, decision points, and scenario-based judgment.
- Do not add interaction merely for novelty.
- Avoid trick questions and superficial recall when an objective requires comparison, interpretation, or decision-making.
- Ensure feedback will explain why an answer is correct or incorrect and will retain supporting citations.

G. Protect accessibility and neutrality

- Design every visual or interactive idea with a text equivalent.
- Do not rely on colour, hover, drag-and-drop, audio, or timed activity as the only way to access information or respond.
- Use vendor-neutral terms such as content block, screen, page, interaction, activity, and assessment item.
- Do not assume a particular LMS, authoring tool, device, or input method.

REQUIRED OUTPUT

Produce an “Instructional Design Blueprint” with the following sections:

1. Course title and one-paragraph design summary
2. Audience, assumed prior knowledge, use context, and qualification boundaries
3. Recommended course duration and scope rationale
4. Content inventory and disposition matrix
   - research topic or claim group;
   - proposed disposition: core, enrichment, reference, or excluded;
   - reason;
   - retained source identifiers or citations.
5. Prerequisite and contextual gap log
   - gap;
   - why learners need it;
   - proposed explanation;
   - original citation or new gap-filling citation;
   - unresolved issue, if any.
6. Four to six measurable learning objectives
7. Objective-to-content-to-assessment alignment matrix
8. Course map
   - section or module;
   - purpose;
   - major content;
   - supporting citations;
   - approximate learning time.
9. Detailed module plans
   For each module include:
   - instructional purpose;
   - prerequisite connection;
   - content sequence;
   - plain-language explanations that will be required;
   - misconceptions to correct;
   - proposed examples, visuals, and interactions;
   - formative practice approach;
   - citations attached to the relevant content.
10. Scenario and application plan
11. Graded-assessment blueprint by objective and cognitive level
12. Proposed glossary and acronym list
13. Citation-integrity report
   - confirm how the original claim-to-source relationships were preserved;
   - list any combined claims and all sources required to support them;
   - list any newly researched content;
   - flag any citation that could not be verified or any claim that remains weakly supported.
14. Open questions and recommendations for the storyboard stage

QUALITY CHECK BEFORE SUBMITTING

Before returning the blueprint, verify that:

- every research topic has a recorded disposition;
- every objective is taught and assessed;
- every factual content cluster retains its citations;
- prerequisite explanations do not introduce uncited facts;
- the proposed language is accessible without becoming inaccurate;
- the scope remains appropriate for educated, nontechnical professionals;
- no platform-specific course-authoring instructions have been introduced;
- no placeholders, fabricated examples, or unsupported claims remain.

Return the complete instructional-design blueprint only. Do not create the final course storyboard until I provide the next prompt.
```
